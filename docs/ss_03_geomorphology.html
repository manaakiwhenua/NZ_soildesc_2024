3  Geomorphology

Modified

September 23, 2026

Geomorphology limits the range of soils that can occur at specific locations on the basis of slope, relief, aspect, and drainage. Geomorphology can be described at multiple scales, which break down into progressively simpler shapes. The system described in this chapter helps the pedologist to do that consistently.

In this system the chosen scales are province (Section 3.1), landscape (Section 3.2), and landform (Section 3.3) as described below. Provinces are large, continuous regions of New Zealand (and its outlying islands) that have a distinctive character driven mostly by their geology and climate. Within provinces, landscapes describe assemblages of spatially related landforms that may form semi-regular patterns. Within landscapes, individual landforms comprise local expressions of earth, air, and water movement over time.

Some additional, highly local geomorphological parameters are captured when describing the land surface at a site (Chapter 10).

3.1 Province

Provinces identify major geomorphic zones in New Zealand, which express large-scale lithological associations, tectonic regimes, and climate. Table 3.1 lists their names, and Figure 3.1 shows their general distribution.

Given their very broad extent, provinces do not need to be recorded in the field. The province boundaries are not currently strictly defined, so an understanding of their conceptual basis (Appendix F) is required before a confident allocation can be made.

3.2 Landscape

The landscape around a soil observation comprises a distinct geomorphological pattern with repeating landforms, or landforms that are otherwise spatially related. The scale of observation varies but commonly extends from a few hundred to a few thousand metres (Heck 2017). Landscapes nest within provinces but are not restricted to one province or another.

3.2.1 Naming landscapes

No universal set of landscape names and definitions exists. The schema given in Table 3.2 is provisional and based on terms seen in common use in New Zealand environmental datasets.

3.2.2 Describing landscapes

A landscape can be described using a short list of parameters: relief, characteristic slope, drainage pattern, structural controls, and the landforms contained within the landscape. These parameters may be useful when mapping at a regional scale but do not need to be routinely recorded in the field.

3.2.2.1 Relief

Relief is the degree of separation between high and low points in a landscape. As described in the Australian Soil and Land Survey Field Handbook (National Committee on Soil and Terrain 2024, p. 36), relief can be visualised by picturing two surfaces roughly parallel to the land surface: one passing through local crests and one through local depressions. The median vertical distance between the two surfaces is the relief.

Relief is usually best determined from a DEM that has been zoned into landscape units. Classification options for relief measurements is presented in Section D.1.3.

3.2.2.2 Characteristic slope

The characteristic slopes are the most common range of slopes within a regional landscape. These are often controlled by local geology. As with relief, characteristic slope is usually best determined from zonal analysis of a DEM-derived slope dataset. Zonal data should be recorded in terms of median and range, in whole degrees. Classification options for slope measurements are presented in Section D.1.1.

Note 3.1: Multiple characteristic slopes

Some landscapes may have two or more distinct characteristic slopes (e.g. the fronts and tops of alluvial terrace landforms on a plain). In this case the median will describe the dominant surface while the range will capture the steeper areas. Recording multiple characteristic slopes at landscape scale is not considered useful because adequate context is provided by naming the landscape and its component landforms.

3.2.2.3 Drainage pattern

Natural drainage patterns are relevant to understanding landscape development but are best observed remotely. They do not need to be recorded in the field. If desired, assess the landscape drainage pattern using available data sources and define it using the attributes given in the Australian Soil and Land Survey Field Handbook (National Committee on Soil and Terrain 2024, p. 38–41).

3.2.2.4 Structural controls

A landscape may be strongly controlled by the structure of the underlying geology, which may in turn influence landforms and patterns of soil formation. These controls are particularly noticeable on erosive sedimentary landscapes. Note them where relevant.

3.2.2.5 Composition

A breakdown of the major landforms expected within the landscape should be included (see Section 3.3 below), along with an estimate of their relative dominance.

3.3 Landform

A landform is a component of the landscape, and again can have a variable extent – usually tens to hundreds of metres. A landform has a relatively simple shape compared to a landscape, and is not itself composed of multiple repeating elements in the way a landscape is. A landform has a characteristic range of slopes and aspects. Landforms also have distinctive composition in terms of geology / parent materials and are usually restricted to particular locations within a landscape.

3.3.1 Naming landforms

As with landscapes, no universal set of landform names and definitions exists. The schema given in Table 3.4 below is provisional and based on terms seen in common use in New Zealand environmental datasets and related publications. An extensive dictionary of landform terminology can also be found in Schoeneberger and Wysocki (2017).

3.3.2 Describing landforms

Where available landform names are unsatisfactory or a more detailed parameterisation of the landform is desired, the landform can be described in terms of its slope, orientation, and element(s).

3.3.2.1 Slope

The characteristic slope of the major part of the landform should be recorded as median and range in whole degrees. Classification systems for slope measurements are presented in Section D.1.1.

3.3.2.2 Orientation

Orientation is the characteristic aspect of the major part of the landform. For slopes this is just the direction the slope faces. For more complex shapes this is the direction of the landform’s longest axis, looking downslope (e.g. the direction in which a valley bottom is draining). In both cases a median aspect and a range should be recorded. Some landforms may not have an identifiable orientation (e.g. small mounds, saddles); in this case orientation should be recorded as not applicable (aspect will still, however, be measurable at site scale, see Section 10.2). Aspect classes may be applied to the recorded data; some options are presented in Section D.1.2.

3.4 Other terrain parameters

The following terrain parameters may be useful in particular contexts.

3.4.1 Slope length

Slope length is used in assessments of erosion vulnerability. Length is measured from the point at which a slope becomes steep enough to lose soil to erosion processes (see Section 10.8.1), to where it levels out enough to receive soils (or to a drainage channel). This is usually impractical to measure in the field, but can be determined computationally given an appropriately detailed DEM. Alternatively, the slope length can be manually traced in GIS. Slope length should be recorded in whole metres.

3.4.2 Exposure

The degree to which a location is exposed or sheltered by the surrounding topography can be measured at multiple scales and may be useful in studies of soil-water behaviour and when completing agricultural suitability assessments.

The Mesoscale Topographic Index (MTI, Hurst et al. (2022)) is a measure of how sheltered or exposed a location is within a landscape. To measure MTI in the field, use a compass and clinometer to measure angle to the horizon at the eight cardinal compass bearings. Record positive values if looking up and negative when looking down. The average of the eight values defines the index, with high values signifying a sheltered area and low values signifying exposure.

Terrain shape index (TSI, Hurst et al. (2022)) is a localised version of MTI, whereby slope angles are measured at cardinal compass points 20 m from the point of observation rather than to the horizon. This measures exposure at the site scale of observation. Average exposure at the scale of the landform can be built from multiple TSI measurements taken across the landform.

For both measures, shelter from trees is usually excluded from the assessment: on-ground measurements are taken to the land surface, the position of which is estimated if the horizon is obscured. Results are reported in units of positive or negative whole degrees.

Tip 3.1: Automating exposure measurements

The closest automated equivalents of the MTI appear to be the Morphometric Protection Index, as implemented in SAGA-GIS (Yokoyama et al. 2002; Conrad et al. 2015), and the ‘intensity’ output of the ‘Geomorphons’ module, as implemented in GRASS-GIS (Jasiewicz and Stepinski 2013; GRASS Development Team 2023). In the case of geomorphon intensity, the output also reports the mean relative elevation in metres of the eight defining points rather than their average angles above or below the central point.

Field- and computer-based measurement of MTI may differ slightly. The in-field version measures against magnetic north rather than ‘map north’, which will vary with spatial data projection. Calculations can also be made from a Digital Surface Model (DSM, a surface that includes, for example, trees and buildings) or the more usual Digital Terrain Model (DTM).

The TSI can also be calculated using the automated tools mentioned above, but will require a DEM with high vertical accuracy and cell size no more than 10 m.
