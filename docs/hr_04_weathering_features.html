15  Weathering features

Modified

October 6, 2026

Weathering features are distinct bodies formed within the soil through pedogenic (natural soil-forming) processes. They contain a higher amount of a particular substance (or association of substances) and thus contrast with the surrounding material. Many of these features create barriers to water movement or alter chemistry in a way that affects soil organisms and plant root growth.

15.1 Concentrations

Concentrations are secondary features that are not associated with specific surfaces within the soil (i.e. they do not form coatings on peds or void spaces, but rather permeate the soil mass; compare with coatings, Section 15.2). Some concentrations contrast sharply with the surrounding soil material due to differences in colour, hardness, or composition, while others may be more subtle.

For rapid description, record the concentration type using Table 15.2 and its percentage abundance. For routine assessment, the form using Table 15.2. For a detailed description, also append size range in millimetres. Colour is not required because it is usually determined by type and may have already been recorded as a colour pattern (Section 13.3). Abundance and size can be classified using Table D.5 and Table D.8 respectively.

For example: use F NO 10% 6-8 mm for scattered small ironstone nodules.

Note 15.1: Lamellae and laminae

Lamellae are thin bands of concentrated but uncemented soil materials, formed by pedological processes. They are usually of a finer texture than their surrounds, and may also have relatively high levels of other weathering products, such as iron precipitates or organic matter. While their formation mechanisms are still not fully understood, the presence of lamellae in a profile can signify the absence of some processes (e.g. bioturbation) and presence of others (e.g. seasonal freeze-thaw activity). Lamellae usually occur horizontally or parallel to the land surface at a scale of several metres, but can exhibit a wavy character at the sub-metre scale.

Lamellae may technically qualify as distinct soil horizons. If a single lamella is evident between two contrasting horizons, it must be described as a separate horizon rather than as a concentration. Where lamellae occur within a larger band of soil material that has consistent composition, they can be described as concentrations.

For example: use LM L 10% 3-5 mm for a series of thin clay bands observed in a larger sandy layer.

For a more detailed discussion of lamellae, see Dijkerman et al. (1967), Rawling (2000), and Bockheim and Hartemink (2013).

By contrast, laminae are thin bands of unconsolidated material with contrasting texture that have been emplaced by water, wind, or pyroclastic activity and have yet to be significantly disturbed by profile development. These geogenic layers are not weathering features and are described using terminology from Section 12.5.3. No minimum or maximum thickness requirements are specified for individual lamina due to lack of data, but anecdotally they tend to be less than 5 cm thick.

15.2 Coatings

Coatings are layers of material that form on the surfaces of other soil features (e.g. grains, peds, or the walls of voids). Some coatings are formed by chemical precipitation, similar to concentrations, while others form as a result of selective removal processes, where the material left behind accumulates. Some coatings may be biological in origin (e.g. some ant species line their nest walls with fine material).

For rapid description, record the major type(s) of coating observed using the codes in Table 15.3, where they are well-developed enough to be obvious. For routine description, for each type append abundance as percentage of soil surfaces covered, and colour using the short codes in Table 13.6 (if colour has not already been recorded as a pattern). For detailed description, record all observable coatings, their respective abundances, and their colours using Munsell colour notation.

For example: use OG D 80% for well-developed, dark-coloured organic coats covering most ped surfaces.

15.3 Pans

Pans form when particular chemical compounds precipitate out of the soil solution and cement a layer of mineral particles (perhaps as a further accumulation and joining together of coatings and/or concentrations), or when particular particle size distributions and/or shape range become very tightly packed. Many pans are diagnostic in the New Zealand Soil Classification because they can form a barrier to plant root growth or water movement (see also Section 19.3).

For rapid description, record pan type using a code from Table 15.4.

For example: use X for a fragipan.

Only record pans that are strongly developed enough to be classification-relevant. A classification-relevant pan is one that is developed enough to meet all of the relevant diagnostic requirements listed in the New Zealand Soil Classification. Many (but not all) pans have dedicated conventional horizon names. The full definitions of those horizons are available in Chapter 18.

For routine description, add continuity (Table 15.5) and structure (Table 15.6).

For example: use FBS for a vesicular iron-pan that has been broken up by deep ripping.

Pans can optionally be described in detail in terms of type, thickness, continuity, structure, strength and cementation. Pans comprise distinct horizons, so their thickness is defined using horizon boundaries. Their strength and cementation are assessed using Table 16.2 and Table 16.10. Codes for type, continuity and structure are given below. Note that continuity can only be observed in large pits or exposures.

15.4 Stress features

Stress features develop along fissures or on ped faces in soils that shrink and swell as water content changes. Stress features are usually shiny but do not differ in colour from the soil matrix. Magnification may be required to observe them clearly. Stress features will not develop in sandy soil textures (see Table 14.2).

For rapid description, optionally record the type of stress feature observed using the codes in Table 15.7.

For routine or detailed soil description, append abundance as percentage of ped faces covered.

For example: use SL 30% in a well-developed Vertic Melanic Soil.

15.5 Cryofeatures

Cryoturbation refers to the physical disturbance and mixing of soil caused by repeated freezing and thawing. This process commonly occurs in soils exposed to seasonal or historical freeze-thaw cycles. Affected soils may show discontinuous horizon boundaries and physical mixing as a result of intense shrink-swell activity caused by ice formation.

Freezing and thawing can also accelerate downslope movement (solifluction), often incorporating angular, poorly sorted rock fragments into the soil. These features can be recognised by examining horizon boundary characteristics (Section 11.3, Section 11.4), colour patterns from mixing (Section 13.3.1), and rock fragment characteristics (Section 12.2.1).
