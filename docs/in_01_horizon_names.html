18  Horizon notation

Modified

September 22, 2026

Pedological horizon notation operates as a shorthand for describing the full profile, making it easier to compare and contrast groups of profiles, and to classify them. Horizons are given a particular designation according to their position in the profile, and according to soil properties that reflect the kind and degree of pedogenic alteration of the parent material.

Becuase designations are made in the field they are defined mainly by morphological properties that differentiate horizons from those above or below, or from a presumed parent material. Laboratory data may be needed to confirm identification, especially in marginal cases.

The current scheme for New Zealand reported here was developed largely from the system used in England and Wales (Avery 1980) by Clayden and Hewitt (2015), and is broadly similar to those used overseas (e.g. the World Reference Base for Soil Resources (IUSS Working Group WRB 2022), Soil Taxonomy (Soil Survey Staff 2022)). However, note that there are significant differences in suffix use between all of these systems as they have become adapted to regional conditions.

18.1 Primary designations

Codes for primary horizon designations (previously known as master horizons) can be used by themselves for rapid assessment, or where criteria for more detailed nomenclature are not met. Always capitalised, they are grouped into two organic horizons (L, O) and five mineral horizons (A, E, B, C, R) as per Table 18.1.

Note 18.1: L Horizons

Note that the L horizon was previously used to describe fresh leaf litter, but such materials are now not considered part of the profile description and so this designation has been removed. Fresh litter presence is described as part of surface cover assessment (Section 10.3). The L horizon now unites the former F and H horizons.

18.3 Redox suffixes

Redox suffixes are used to signify the degree to which a horizon is affected by reduction due to persistent waterlogging, as evidenced by both the matrix colour (Section 13.2) and the degree of development of redox segregations.

Redox segregations are formed as a result of the reduction and solubilisation of iron and/or manganese, their translocation and concentration, and their re-oxidation and precipitation in the form of oxides. They may comprise any of the following: redox mottles (Section 13.3), concentrations, coatings, and pans (Chapter 15).

Rule for use follow.

  • Only choose one redox suffix per horizon, if needed (e.g. Bg); the definitions are mutually exclusive.
  • g and r suffixes cannot be used with Ea, Ew or Bw horizons because they are partly defined by their development under well-aerated conditions and so will not have enough low-chroma colours in the matrix or on ped faces to qualify.
  • only the g suffix can be added to A horizons, in cases where ≥2% redox segregations occur. This is because A horizons are expected to be low-chroma from elevated organic matter, so do not meet the requirements for (f) or (g) suffixes. The use of the g suffix in A horizons may not necessarily signify strong reduction.

For further discussion about assessing redox intensity in soils, see Section 19.1. Table 18.8 summarises the colour and redox segregation requirements in Table 18.7 for quick reference.

18.4 Additional suffixes

One or more of these suffixes can used to provide more specific information about a horizon. They are placed after any redox suffixes in use For example, a Bg horizon containing more than 5% by volume of concretions is designated Bgc.

18.5 Transitional horizons

Two main kinds of transitional horizons are distinguished by specific conventions.

  • Transitional horizons having properties of two master horizons, as in gradational horizons in vertical sequence (e.g. Ap-AB-Bw). In other cases a horizon can be designated as transitional even if one of the master horizons to which it is apparently transitional is not present. For example, an AB horizon can be recognised where the underlying layer is bedrock (R), and a BC horizon can be recognised even if no underlying C horizon is identified. Lower-case suffixes (see Section 18.3 and Section 18.4) are used following the two capital letters where the property denoted by the suffix applies to the whole horizon (e.g. ABg, BCt, BCx).
  • Transitional horizons in which discrete parts have recognisable properties of two kinds of master horizon (e.g. where pieces of A horizon are incorporated in a B horizon, or where horizons interfinger). In this case the horizon is designated by two capital letters separated by a solidus (e.g. B/C). Appropriate suffixes should be used where the components of the material are clearly identifiable (e.g. Ap/Bw).

For simplicity the capital letters for all transitional horizons are used in the order A, E, B, C, irrespective of which properties dominate. For example, all horizons transitional between A and B horizons are designated AB or A/B, with suffixes as appropriate.

18.6 Numbering similar horizons

If multiple horizons with the same name but minor differences in morphology are evident in a profile, distinguish the horizons by appending them with a number, in the order in which they are encountered down the profile. The horizon names must otherwise be identical; for example, Bt1, Bt2, Btg1, Btg2 is correct, but Bt1, Bt2, Btg3, Btg4 is not. This convention can, however, cross lithological discontinuities (e.g. Bw1, Bw2, 2Bw3, 2Bw4). The convention can also be interrupted (e.g. Ah1, Bt, bAh2). The numerical suffix must always be placed last in the name.

18.7 Lithological discontinuities

A numbered prefix may be used to group horizons within lithological or stratigraphic zones separated by discontinuities. Such discontinuities are identified by a significant difference between adjacent horizons in the particle-size distribution or in mineralogy, which cannot be attributed to pedogenic processes. The main purpose of recognising lithological differences is to draw attention to inherited layering that can significantly influence physical properties, particularly permeability, as well as providing insight into the landscape and land-use history that can be represented by a multilayered profile.

Horizons comprising the uppermost parent material layer are not specifically designated ‘1’, as many profiles contain no discontinuities and so the information would usually be superfluous. The subsequent layers are numbered 2, 3, etc. consecutively with depth, incrementing at each stratigraphic break. Each number may be applied to multiple adjacent horizons, until and unless another discontinuity is encountered. A change in parent material origin class (see Section 11.8.2) may often signify a discontinuity, but multiple discontinuities can exist within a sequence of the same parent material origin class, particularly in transported materials. 18.8 Buried horizons

Pedogenic horizons that have been buried by the deposition of additional material and little modified subsequently are called buried horizons and denoted using the prefix ‘b’. A buried horizon is obviously not in the same deposit as overlying horizons. Some buried horizons, however, are formed in material that is lithologically similar to the overlying deposit. The symbol is not used in Organic Soils or to separate an organic layer from a mineral layer.

Buried soil horizons, even when only very weakly weathered, mark soil formation that took place through top-down processes when that parent material was at the land surface. They represent disconformities and carry information on landscape evolution, climate, and time. The boundary between the top of a buried soil and an overlying tephra or other geological deposit is a paraconformity, marking a period of non-deposition (Palmer et al. 2025).

The notation for a buried Ah horizon in material similar to the overlying deposit is bAh, and that for a buried Ah horizon in dissimilar material is 2bAh. The horizons of a sequence of buried soils are given consecutive numbers for each soil following the prefix b, independently of the numbers for lithological discontinuities that are placed before the b. 18.9 Anthropogenic horizons

Horizons formed in anthropogenic soil materials are denoted using the prefix ‘e’ (e.g. eAp). This may include deliberately manufactured soils (e.g. for sports fields or mine rehabilitation), or natural soils that have a significant component of anthropogenic modification (e.g. backyard soils, kūmara fields). The remaining horizons of a truncated profile should not receive this designation, nor should agricultural topsoils that have only been subject to low-impact and reversible practices like tillage or fertilisation.

Given that most anthropogenic soils in New Zealand will be less than about 800 years old, it is considered unlikely at present that the ‘e’ prefix will be valid when applied to subsoil horizons that require long periods of time to form (e.g. Bo).
